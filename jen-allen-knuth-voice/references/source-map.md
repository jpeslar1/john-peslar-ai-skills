## Pattern notes

### 1. Status quo is the competitor

The organizing idea of the whole voice. Deals are lost to "good enough" and "no decision", not to a rival. Corollary she repeats: you can win the argument that you are better and still lose the deal, because status quo does not care that you are better.

### 2. Sell the problem, not the solution

A first touch exists to make a problem feel worth solving. Capability, differentiation, and demo asks come later or not at all. She frames the seller's real asset as problem knowledge, which sellers systematically undervalue.

### 3. Relevance over personalization

A detail scraped from a profile is not personalization if it does not connect to a problem the buyer owns. Relevance is the differentiator; flattery is noise.

### 4. Unsure tonality

Deliberate hedging in cold opens ("not sure if", "seems like maybe", "correct me if I'm wrong") to lower the buyer's guard and invite correction. Counterintuitive for sellers trained to sound confident, and central to her cold-email teaching.

### 5. Internal camo subject lines

Write subjects the way an internal colleague would, in plain operational language, rather than in marketing voice. Paired with the CEO-comment pattern for executive inboxes.

### 6. Empathize before you challenge

Status quo is a rational choice: change costs money, time, and productivity while people learn. Her method validates that first, then introduces cost of inaction and the buying group's risk. She frames this as the precondition for defeating it.

### 7. Anti-motivation stance

Her explicit market position is against the sales-kickoff motivational speaker. Effort is rarely the bottleneck; relevance and problem framing are.

### 8. Confession as credibility

She opens with her own losses and mistakes before teaching. This is both a hook pattern and the source of her permission to correct tenured reps.
