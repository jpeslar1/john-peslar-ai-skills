# David Ogilvy Voice Corpus

Research date: 2026-09-09

The excerpts below are short quotations from widely published books, a widely reproduced internal memo, and a documented advertisement, included under fair use for style analysis and attribution. They are third-party content and NOT covered by this repository's MIT license.

**Provenance warning, read this.** No primary text was fetched for this corpus. Ogilvy's books are in print and were not read for this build, and the 1982 memo was read through secondary reproductions. **Every item below is labeled `documented`, meaning it is widely republished and consistently attributed, but not verified against the printed page in this session.** Check any line against the book before reproducing it as a direct quotation in client work. See `source-map.md`.

## Documented excerpts

### On the reader

1. "The consumer isn't a moron. She is your wife."
   Attributed to 1955 and published in Confessions of an Advertising Man (1963). `documented`. Note: quote this in historical context. Its framing is of its era and should not be used as live audience guidance.

2. "I do not regard advertising as entertainment or an art form, but as a medium of information."
   Attributed to Ogilvy on Advertising. `documented`

3. "I don't know the rules of grammar. If you're trying to persuade people to do something, or buy something, it seems to me you should use their language."
   Widely attributed. `documented`

### On headlines and copy

4. "On the average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar."
   Attributed to Confessions of an Advertising Man and repeated in Ogilvy on Advertising. `documented`

5. "What you say in advertising is more important than how you say it."
   Attributed to Confessions of an Advertising Man. `documented`

6. "Never write an advertisement which you wouldn't want your own family to read."
   Widely attributed to Confessions of an Advertising Man. `documented`, attribution repeated everywhere but not verified here.

7. "If it doesn't sell, it isn't creative."
   Widely attributed to him and used as an agency standard. `documented`, wording varies across sources.

### From the 1982 internal memo "How to Write"

Reproduced in many places, including The Unpublished David Ogilvy (1986). The lines below are the ones that appear consistently across reproductions.

8. "The better you write, the higher you go in Ogilvy & Mather. People who think well, write well."
   `documented`

9. "Woolly minded people write woolly memos, woolly letters and woolly speeches."
   `documented`

10. "Good writing is not a natural gift. You have to learn to write well."
    `documented`

11. "Write the way you talk. Naturally."
    `documented`

12. "Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs."
    `documented`

13. "Never use jargon words like reconceptualize, demassification, attitudinally, judgmentally. They are hallmarks of a pretentious ass."
    `documented`. The named offenders matter; this is the taboo list in his own words.

14. "Never write more than two pages on any subject."
    `documented`. Note the tension with his advocacy of long body copy: the memo rule governs internal memos, not advertisements.

15. "Check your quotations."
    `documented`. Worth noting that this corpus itself is the reason for that rule.

16. "Never send a letter or a memo on the day you write it."
    `documented`

17. "Before you send your letter or your memo, make sure it is crystal clear what you want the recipient to do."
    `documented`

18. "If you want ACTION, don't write. Go and tell the guy what you want."
    `documented`

### The advertisement most often cited as his

25. "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock"
    Rolls-Royce, 1958. `documented`

    **Important provenance detail, and the reason this ad belongs in a voice corpus at all:** by his own account the line was not invented by him. He spent weeks reading about the car and took the sentence from a quote by the technical editor of a British motor magazine. He then wrote 26 headline candidates and had other writers in the agency pick the winner. The lesson is the method, not the sentence: research until you find the fact, write many headlines, let others choose.

## Structural notes for the writer

- The rules he wrote for memos and the rules he wrote for advertisements are different. Two pages maximum is a memo rule. Advertisements get as much length as the facts justify.
- The strongest lines are the flattest. "She is your wife." "Check your quotations." No decoration.
- He names the offending word rather than describing the offence. "reconceptualize, demassification, attitudinally, judgmentally."
- Proportions are his favourite evidence: five times as many, eighty cents out of your dollar, 26 headlines.
- The famous headline came from research, not from inspiration. Any imitation of this voice that skips the research step is imitating the surface only.
