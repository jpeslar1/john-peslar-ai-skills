# Sam Parr Voice Corpus

Research date: 2026-09-09

The excerpts below are short quotations from a public article he authored on thehustle.co and from public video interviews and talks, included under fair use for style analysis and attribution. They are third-party content and NOT covered by this repository's MIT license.

**Provenance note.** Items 1-29 were read directly from thehustle.co on the research date and are verbatim from a piece signed "Sam, CEO of The Hustle"; item 30 is subscribe-block copy from the same property. Items 1-30 are `verified`. Items 31-50 are spoken excerpts pulled from four YouTube videos on the research date, transcribed by auto-generated (ASR) caption tracks with no human speaker labels; every line was checked against its source transcript and kept only where the surrounding exchange makes Sam Parr unambiguously the speaker. Items 31-50 are `documented`, not `verified`, because ASR wording can be imperfect even where attribution is certain. His newsletter issues and X posts were not sampled; see `source-map.md`, which flags this as the corpus's main remaining weakness.

## Verbatim excerpts

### From "How to write like The Hustle" (thehustle.co, signed Sam, CEO of The Hustle)

Source: https://thehustle.co/write-like-hustle-boring-stuff-writing-no-one-talks

1. "So, instead of the normal news, we're giving you the kick in the pants to put some quality content out there that people actually want to read, and address something many of you folks ask us about: how to write in The Hustle voice."

2. "Below is the step-by-step process John and I used to launch The Hustle back in the day, which allowed us to produce lots of high-quality content on a daily basis (sometimes 3-6 blog posts a day each!) - all without formal writing backgrounds."

3. "Let's do this. / - Sam, CEO of The Hustle"
   The sign-off with a role attached.

4. "1. Write the headline / It all starts here. Writing the headline first forces you to hone in on what you want to talk about. Make the headline 3-10 words. Write the headline in plain English as if you're describing what the article's about to a friend."

5. "Example: I went 30 days with no food, living off only Soylent"
   His own headline, used as the running example through the piece.

6. "2. Write the description / One or two sentences that describe what the article is about."

7. "3. Write your draft / This is the toughest part. Why? Most people are deathly afraid of writing and having their thoughts read."

8. "Accept that it will suck. That's ok. Just start writing. Write anything. "I don't know how to start, but I'm just starting now." Literally write that."

9. "Motion creates emotion. Accept that this is a dump and that it'll suck."

10. "4. Incubate / Take whatever time you have to walk away from your work. Could be 2 minutes or 2 years. Whatever. Just get away from your work."

11. "Ironically, not focusing on a problem actually helps you come up with new ideas - it's called fixation forgetting (#showerthoughts)."

12. "5. Edit / This is where the magic happens. David Ogilvy, the greatest copywriter in the world, once said, "I'm a lousy copywriter, but I am a good editor." Editing is where we turn crap into gold."
    Note the inline citation of Ogilvy. The Ogilvy line belongs to Ogilvy.

13. "6. Cut / The first part of editing is cutting useless words and sentences. For most people, that means cutting the first 25% and last 25%."

14. "Since grade school, we've been programmed to write to hit a certain word count. / That's wrong. Every single word must be necessary. Nothing more, nothing less."

15. "Don't use 1,500 words to explain something if you need 500. Too many words dilutes your message."

16. "7. First sentence / Next, make the first sentence punch the reader in the face. First sentences need to cut through to the reader. In a world where people's attention is hard to attract, the first sentence must be undeniable."

17. "8. Create the slippery slope / The sole purpose of the first sentence is to get you to read the second sentence. The sole purpose of the second sentence is to get you to read the third sentence… and so on."

18. "Lead with a personal story, give hints about what's coming later, or pose a question. Give people a reason to keep reading."

19. "Example: "I spent the last 30 days eating nothing but Soylent, a new age powdered meal replacement. Why would I do something so stupid? I'll explain. But first, if you aren't familiar with Soylent, here's the gist.""
    His own opening, and the clearest single sample of the voice in motion.

20. "9. Write simple / Around a 7th to 8th-grade reading level. A great post has C+ writing and A+ storytelling, insight, or analysis."

21. "Contrary to what your 5th grade English teacher used to say, don't use flowery, overly complicated language."

22. "10. Use short sentences, paragraphs and simple words / A general rule is to keep paragraphs around 2-4 sentences. Keep sentences around 25 words or less."

23. "In his famous Letters to Shareholders, where Warren Buffett writes about the complex insurance industry, Buffett averages 13 words per sentence. Even the most complex topics can be simplified."

24. "11. Don't use too many adverbs / Adverbs (words that end in "ly") weaken your words. I yelled at the boy is better than I angrily yelled at the boy. The road to hell, Stephen King once wrote, is paved with adverbs."
    The King line belongs to Stephen King.

25. "12. Go back to your headline / 90% of people will read your headline without consuming your article. Yet, people rarely spend time perfecting their headline. Write 25 of them."

26. "When you have your headline written, you have spent ninety cents out of your dollar."
    A restatement of a classic direct-response maxim, in his phrasing.

27. "14. Copywork / And finally…need inspiration? Do copywork. Find a blogger, author, or poet you like and write out their work by hand. See how they structure their writing. Feel their texture."

28. "I spent two hours a day for 6 months copying my favorite authors. It worked wonderfully."
    His personal receipt. Not transferable to the user.

29. "Ok. That's all…now go write!"
    The closing register: casual, ellipsis, no ceremony.

## Structural notes for the writer

- The craft rules he teaches are the rules his own prose obeys. Sentences in this corpus almost never exceed 25 words.
- Numbers appear as numerals with units: 3-10 words, 25%, 1,500 words, 13 words per sentence, 90%, 25 headlines, 2.5M.
- Influences are cited inline by name: Ogilvy, Buffett, Stephen King. He does not pretend the method is his invention.
- Jokes and asides live inside the instruction, in parentheses or as a fragment, not appended at the end.
- The example carries the teaching. He picks one piece, the Soylent post, and refers back to it throughout.

## Structural notes for the spoken register

- He talks in long strung-together clauses joined by "and" and "so". His 25-word rule governs his prose, not his speech.
- Filler is constant: like, um, uh, cuz, kind of, pretty much. Strip it when writing, but know the drafts he speaks are messy before he cuts.
- He hedges counts ("250 or 350") and then states money precisely ("50 grand"). The precision lands on the dollar figure, never on the crowd size.
- Self-deprecation arrives before every claim of expertise. "I am not a writer" precedes an hour of writing instruction.
- Analogies come from music, TV, boxing, and piano lessons, not from business. The teaching is always ported through something physical.
- Profanity is casual and frequent in conversation and gets asterisked or bleeped only in published brand copy.
