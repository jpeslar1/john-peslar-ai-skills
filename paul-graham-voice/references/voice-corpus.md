# Paul Graham Voice Corpus

Research date: 2026-10-19

The excerpts below are short quotations from public posts, articles, and talks, included under fair use for style analysis and attribution. They are third-party content and NOT covered by this repository's MIT license.

**Provenance note.** All 26 items are verbatim and written by him. Items 1-23 and 26 are copied from the plain public HTML of thirteen essays on paulgraham.com, dated by his own month-and-year datelines (February 2007 to September 2026); where an item is a closing paragraph or sits under a section heading, the source line says so. Items 24-25 are his own Hacker News comments (username pg, 2019 and 2024), read from the public Hacker News Firebase API with second-level timestamps. Nothing here comes from a quote aggregator. No X posts are included: the @paulg syndication timeline rate-limited on two attempts and no status IDs were located, so his short-form register is unsampled. No spoken transcript was pulled; How to Earn a Billion Dollars (item 21-22) is his own written version of an Oxford Union talk and is the only talk-derived material, labelled verbatim because he wrote it up himself.

**Punctuation note.** No em or en dashes appear in the 26 sampled excerpts; the research notes record that he uses em dashes occasionally but sparingly in the full essays. The operator's copy rule still bans em and en dashes in anything generated in this voice; see the Punctuation Override in SKILL.md.

## Verbatim excerpts (paulgraham.com essays, 2007-2026)

1. "Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off. There may be a handful that just grew by themselves, but usually it takes some sort of push to get them going."

2. "At YC we use the term "Collison installation" for the technique they invented. More diffident founders ask "Will you try our beta?" and if the answer is yes, they say "Great, we'll send you a link." But the Collison brothers weren't going to wait. When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say "Right then, give me your laptop" and set them up on the spot."
    Do Things that Don't Scale, paulgraham.com, July 2013, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html, verbatim

3. "And most importantly, if you have to work hard to delight users when you only have a handful of them, you'll keep doing it when you have a lot."

4. "The following recipe assumes you're very ambitious."

5. "The work you choose needs to have three qualities: it has to be something you have a natural aptitude for, that you have a deep interest in, and that offers scope to do great work."

## Derived stats (honest estimates from this corpus)

- Median sentence length: ~15 words; the range is narrow, with few sentences under 6 or over 30 words, and almost no fragments.
- One-line paragraphs: rare in the body (under 10%); the closing line of an essay is often a single aphoristic sentence ("That's what you do when life is short.").
- Hook-type frequency across the 26 excerpts: plain declarative thesis in sentence one ("Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off.") ~35%, a received saying quoted then denied ("'You get out,' I heard a thousand times, 'what you put in.' They meant well, but this is rarely true.") ~15%, hedged prediction ("I'm usually reluctant to make predictions... but") ~15%, anecdote that is the argument (the Chesky talk, the Collison installation) ~20%, a one-line definition ("A wise person is someone who usually knows the right thing to do.") ~15%.
- Hedges: the signature, in about 1 of 3 excerpts: "as far as I know", "There are exceptions, but remarkably few", "I feel fairly confident", "usually", "rarely", "Curiously enough". The hedge sits next to the strongest claim, not the weakest.
- Exclamation marks: zero in the 26 excerpts. ALL CAPS: zero. Emoji: zero. Hashtags: zero. Bold or italics: none observed in essay bodies.
- Profanity: 1 of 26 ("Relentlessly prune bullshit"), used as a plain noun; "so annoying" is as hot as the register gets.
- Numbers: small, concrete, and arithmetical rather than statistical: 52 weekends, a 2 year old, half as good, a couple decades, a handful, a thousand times; no percentages in the sample.
- Named examples: real companies and people by name, used as evidence (Stripe, the Collison brothers, Brian Chesky, Ron Conway, Steve Jobs, John Sculley); new terms are flagged with quotation marks once ("Collison installation", "founder mode") and then used bare.
- Contractions: throughout (you'll, don't, it's, there's, won't); vocabulary is short Anglo-Saxon words, with the long careful argument carried by "But", "And", "Actually", and "So" at sentence starts.
- Favorite openers: a dateline, then a declarative thesis in the first or second sentence; "It struck me recently how..."; a conditional stated as a recipe ("The following recipe assumes you're very ambitious.").
- Favorite closers: a one-sentence restatement that reads as an aphorism ("There will still be smart people, but only those who choose to be."; "It's always good when money flows through you."); then a Notes section with numbered footnotes and a "Thanks to ... for reading drafts of this" line (research notes); never a call to action.
- Register across channels: the Hacker News comments (items 24-25) use the same plain hedged argument with parenthetical (a) and (b) enumeration, only shorter; there is no separate casual register.
- Spoken register: unsampled. Item 21 shows the talk-derived voice adds direct address to the room ("I'm going to tell you how people become billionaires") while keeping essay syntax.
