# George Mack Voice Corpus

Research date: 2026-10-19

The excerpts below are short quotations from public posts, articles, and talks, included under fair use for style analysis and attribution. They are third-party content and NOT covered by this repository's MIT license.

**Provenance note.** All 34 items are verbatim. Items 1-14 and 21 are copied from the raw static HTML of essays on his own site, george-mack.com (the pages carry no publication date; they are dated only by site liveness at research time). Items 15-20 are from highagency.com, his long essay "High Agency in 30 Minutes", which he announced on X on 2025-03-24. Items 22-31 are his own X posts (@george__mack): root tweets were fetched and dated via fxtwitter, two tweet IDs came from links embedded on his own site, and mid-thread tweets (items 23-25) came from Thread Reader unrolls of the same threads (marked inline; they rest on Thread Reader's copy, not an individual re-fetch). Items 32-34 are LinkedIn posts whose permalinks render logged out; text is from the page meta description and dates from JSON-LD. Nothing here comes from a quote aggregator. No spoken sample was collected (podcast appearances exist but no transcript was fetched), his weekly ideas threads were not pulled because the X timeline endpoint was rate-limited, and Ad Professor's site and any newsletter were not fetched. British spellings (behaviour, humour) are his.

**Punctuation note.** Several website excerpts preserve em dashes because they are verbatim quotations (items 10, 11, 12, 16, 20), and his X and LinkedIn posts use a double hyphen ("--") as the dash (items 23, 26, 28, 30, 33, 34); items 21 and 27 use a spaced single hyphen. All are kept as written. The operator's copy rule still bans em and en dashes in anything generated in this voice; see the Punctuation Override in SKILL.md.

## Derived stats (honest estimates from this corpus)

- Median sentence length: ~12 words in essays and ~14 on X; one-sentence beats of 2-4 words recur ("Adult syndrome.", "Let's go...", "No.").
- One-line paragraphs: roughly 45% of essay paragraphs are a single sentence; the rest are 2-3 sentences built from a micro-anecdote plus a turn.
- Hook-type frequency across the 34 excerpts: forced-choice question or thought experiment ~25% (jail cell, double your luck, who do you call); cascade of vivid historical or statistical micro-examples ~25% (Mozart, Jobs, blackened teeth, wheels on suitcases, 17 teaspoons); coined label plus definition ~25% (Adult Syndrome, Luck razor, Trojan Horses, the normal paradox, dead vs alive problem); numbered list entry with a one-line rule ~20%; personal announcement or credential ~5%.
- Numbered lists: 8 of 34 excerpts are numbered items ("4.", "9.", "13.", "14."); essays are segmented 1. / 2. / 3. with short sections.
- Rhetorical question answered at once: present in about a third of excerpts ("Does it make them less impressive? No. It makes them more impressive." / "Who do you call? Why did you choose this person?").
- Emoji: zero in 34 excerpts. Hashtags: zero. Exclamation marks: 1 ("I finished my essay!").
- Caps for emphasis: none; emphasis is carried by asterisks once ("*these* tweets") and by the one-line turn.
- Profanity: zero. Named living targets: zero; the examples are historical figures, types, or himself.
- Hedges: light. "I think" appears twice ("I think agency might be the most important idea") and "what I call" once; otherwise flat declaratives and paradox statements.
- Numbers: specific and vivid rather than round (17 teaspoons, $104,755, 30lbs, 200,000 lives, 7 months, 9 months, 1% vs 99%, 25 and 75); used as examples, not as proof of his own results.
- Favorite openers: "Thought experiment:", "You wake up in…", "Who is the most…", "My problem with the term…", "Fun story:", a borrowed quote followed by "Why?" (noted in the research, not sampled).
- Favorite closers: a one-line turn or inversion ("Assume everything is a game of skill.", "Worry hates an away game.", "Gas shrinks to the size of the container."), "Let's go..." or "Let's go down the … rabbit hole" as a pivot into the body, "Steal this thought" pull-quotes on the site, and the adprofessor.com CTA on every LinkedIn post.
- Spoken vs written: no spoken sample. The site essays, X threads, and LinkedIn lists are one register; LinkedIn is the same voice applied to ad teardowns with a sales CTA.
