# Fletch (Anthony Pierri and Robert Kaminski) Voice Corpus

Research date: 2026-10-19

The excerpts below are short quotations from public posts, articles, and talks, included under fair use for style analysis and attribution. They are third-party content and NOT covered by this repository's MIT license.

**Provenance note.** All 31 items are verbatim written text, and every source line names the speaker. Items 1-10 are Anthony Pierri's own LinkedIn posts (three long argumentative posts from 2023 located by search, plus October 2026 posts and one image caption), read from logged-out permalinks with the full post text and a machine-readable publish date. Items 11-22 are Rob Kaminski's own LinkedIn posts (September-October 2026; his handle is heyrobk), read the same way. Items 23-29 are from The Fletch Newsletter on beehiiv (three essays, February-April 2026), published under the house pseudonym Fletcher Fletchington, so they are attributed to Fletch rather than to either founder. Items 30-31 are house copy from the fletchpmm.com homepage and FAQ page, fetched on the research date. Typographic apostrophes were normalized to straight ASCII; wording, line breaks (rendered as slashes), CAPS, doubled punctuation, and emoji are otherwise untouched. Nothing here comes from a quote aggregator. There are no documented-grade items: podcast appearances (Exit Five, MKT1) were not transcribed, and the 600-plus before/after pages were not excerpted because they are rewritten client copy rather than the founders' own prose. Items 101-110 are a separate documented-grade spoken sample from one 2026 podcast episode with both founders (The Marketer's Exit, Ep. 35), machine-transcribed with speaker diarization; each item names its speaker, identified from context in every 20-minute chunk.

**Punctuation note.** Item 9 preserves an em dash because it is a verbatim quotation; it is the only dash in the sample, which otherwise runs on ellipses, arrows, and line breaks. The operator's copy rule still bans em and en dashes in anything generated in this voice; see the Punctuation Override in SKILL.md.

## Derived stats (honest estimates from this corpus)

- Median sentence length: ~11 words on LinkedIn, where almost every sentence is its own line; newsletter sentences run ~15 words with short declarative stacks ("Tools are small. Point solutions. Easy to replace. Low price tag.").
- One-line paragraphs: about 90% of LinkedIn lines stand alone with a blank line between; in the newsletter roughly a third of paragraphs are one sentence.
- Hook-type frequency across the 31 items: founder or market quote then diagnosis ~25%, bet or forced choice ~15%, parody framework or cheatsheet ~15%, permission or when-is-it-safe question ~15%, caution flag or problem label ~15%, flat definition ("Positioning is...") ~15%.
- Ellipsis bridges: present in about half of the LinkedIn posts, at line end and line start ("Okay fair enough... / ...but is it possible"); near zero in the newsletter.
- CAPS for a stressed word: roughly one per LinkedIn post (SUPER, MAIN, DO NOT, EVEN BETTER, BUT, LEAST); whole-line CAPS only for parody titles ("THE OBESE STARTUP METHODOLOGY").
- Emoji: about one per Rob post as a section marker (🚩 ☝️ 🙃 🤯), fewer in Anthony's posts, none in the newsletter prose; hashtags: none in the sample (noted on Rob's older posts, unsampled).
- Arrows and labels: "→" and "↳" sub-points, "Option 1 / Option 2", "Objection 1", "Step 1/2/3", "Problem 1" appear in roughly 40% of items.
- Profanity: one "pissed off" (Anthony); no named-person insults; the target is "90% of B2B homepages" or an ad campaign the writer is a customer of.
- Hedges: rare and strategic ("is it possible that", "One of these options seems more likely to me", "Probably."); confidence is the default, up to inviting disagreeing readers to unsubscribe.
- Numbers: blunt and few; $1M ARR, $203B, 90% of B2B homepages, 99% sure, 5 different versions of PMF, 100 page slide PowerPoint, 3 questions.
- Favorite openers: a quoted founder line; "Let's make a bet:"; "This is going to sound very..."; "A caution to SaaS startup founders..."; "When is it 'safe' to...".
- Favorite closers: a deadpan one-worder ("Probably.", "That's it."), a mirror pair, a forced choice left open, or an event or newsletter CTA on Anthony's posts.
- Speaker split: Anthony is conversational and heckling ("btw", "Anyways", "old man yells at cloud"); Rob is framework-and-mirror with emoji markers and numbered logic; the newsletter blends both into a longer parable with a third-party stat. Spoken register: see the next bullet.
- Spoken vs written (items 101-110, one 2026 podcast with both founders): spoken sentences run 20-40 words against ~11 on LinkedIn, with 'like', 'you know', and 'right?' throughout. Anthony (items 101-103, 106-107, 109) tells long self-deprecating stories and argues by analogy (Shark Tank discovery questions), while Rob (items 104-105, 108, 110) states the thesis flat ('the thesis was we need to get specific') and closes on a deadpan line ('You're the big baby.'). Casual profanity appears on air from Rob ('didn't know shit', 'a bunch of shit') against one 'pissed off' in the written sample.
