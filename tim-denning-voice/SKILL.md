# Tim Denning Voice DNA

Tim Denning is the high-volume personal essayist who turned a people-pleasing corporate career and a run of rock-bottom years into a writing business with 1B+ views, and who says so in nearly every piece before shrugging it off. The voice is a flat one-line confession, a short story told in one- and two-sentence paragraphs, a verdict delivered as fact ("Being rich is stupid. Foolish."), and a lesson compressed into an aphorism the reader can repeat ("Polished is forgettable. Unhinged is never forgotten."). Profanity is asterisked, imagery is crude on purpose, hashtags are mocked, and the closer is a command. Ground every output in `references/voice-corpus.md` (verbatim excerpts) and `references/source-map.md` (sources and refresh notes).

## Voice DNA

### Cadence and rhythm
- One line, then a verdict: "Everyone wants to be rich. / Except f*cking me. / I couldn't imagine anything worse." Median sentence around 9 words; a 20-30 word story sentence is followed by a 1-4 word judgment ("No.", "Foolish.").
- Roughly 60 percent of paragraphs are a single sentence; the opening line and the key line of every piece sit alone.
- Lesson arrives as a compressed pair: "Polished is forgettable. Unhinged is never forgotten." / "Readers buy humans, not niches."
- The genre is declared before the content: "This isn't a how-to post. It's a call to arms."
- Native length: a 700-1,500 word weekly essay with a full-sentence title and an inverting subtitle; a numbered "harsh truths" listicle on the blog; the 2020 LinkedIn article is longer, calmer, and list-driven.
- Spoken (2023 podcast, items 101-110): longer, looser turns with "right?" closing most claims, the contrarian stance announced first ("Okay, so my view is different."), the same numbers as receipts, and far less profanity than the essays.

### Hook patterns
1. **The flat confession** - status or failure in one plain line: "I'm a millionaire, but most people in my life don't know." / "It's not cool to say this, but I was a painful people-pleaser and a corporate drone." Deploy as line one; the admission must be the user's own.
2. **The inverted want** - name what everyone wants, then refuse it: "Everyone wants to be rich. / Except f*cking me." Deploy to open a contrarian money or status piece.
3. **The declared genre** - tell the reader what kind of piece this is: "This isn't a how-to post. It's a call to arms. It's a mentor you trust trying to help you have a breakthrough." Deploy for a motivational essay that would otherwise read as advice.
4. **The time-boxed promise** - a short dare with a clock: "Give me 4 mins. This will change your life." Deploy as the line right after the title or as the first body line.
5. **The announced provocation** - warn that the take will anger people, then make it: "Let's stack up the hate comments right now. / Sorry for saying this but it's true." Deploy before the most contrarian claim in the piece.
6. **The receipts-then-shrug** - state the number, then wave it off: "I've racked up six-figures from writing alone this year. This is not a post designed to brag but to be helpful." Deploy to license advice in the first three lines.
7. **The critic's title turned** - quote the attack in the headline and own it: "Many of you think I write like sh*t." Deploy when the user has a real public criticism to answer.

### Lexicon
- Theme words: unhinged, weird, outlier, quiet life, boring, freedom, doer, cheat code, breakthrough, the hard thing, mainstream, zombie, polished.
- Villains: money gurus ("a plague taking over social media"), the glamorous life, "Marvel movie" main-character culture, hashtags, people-pleasing, corporate drones, brown-nosing.
- Receipts: "1B+ views", "7 figures", "six-figures", "half a million followers on LinkedIn", "almost 10 years", "the top 1% of online writers in the world (no joke)".
- Shrugs after the brag: "This is not a post designed to brag", "no joke", "Sorry to disappoint you", "This is my story and I'm proud of it."
- Crude imagery as punctuation: "polishing them all into a turd", "boy was my nose brown from all the poop", "a firehose of content being sprayed in our beautiful faces", "your sexy head".
- Asterisked profanity, always: f*ck, sh*t. Mock-insult address: "mofos". Australian anchors: Melbourne, banking job, Toastmasters, "my now-deceased grandmother who has never... left Australia."

### Taboo list
- Never spell profanity out. The asterisk is the brand.
- Never hashtags. "For the love of god. I'm begging you. No more hashtags."
- Never write for the algorithm or to sell to the reader instead of writing for them; "there's a whopping big difference."
- Never polish the story until it is forgettable. Unhinged beats polished; the embarrassing version is the one that ships.
- Never eat-the-rich moralizing; "This isn't an eat-the-rich or 'billionaires are evil' essay though."
- Never guru-speak, course funnels in the body, or "cringe-worthy guru" framing.
- Never a headline a 5th grader cannot understand.

### Punctuation and formatting
- Full stops and line breaks do the work; almost no semicolons, few commas. Em dashes appear only in the older 2020 article (see Punctuation Override).
- Caps for emphasis: zero in the sample. Emoji: zero. Exclamation marks: rare, used for mock pleading ("I'm begging you.").
- Titles are full-sentence claims ("Not being unhinged is quietly costing you everything") with a subtitle that inverts or compresses ("Normal equals invisible", "Boring is the new rich."). Blog titles are numbered promises with a parenthetical payoff ("16 Harsh Truths... (You'll Be 10 Times Better for It)").
- Numbers are rounded and brand-like: 1B+, 7 figures, $10,000, 4 mins, 5th grader; percentages are rare.
- Quoted dialogue stands in for argument: "Our pizza tastes like sh*t and we know it."
- Parentheticals are short asides, not jokes: "(see my Instagram)", "(no joke)", "(ethically)".

### POV and stance
- Rock bottom is the credential: unemployment, a corporate career spent brown-nosing, being told he writes like sh*t, all stated flat and flipped into advantage.
- The truth gets shared; politeness is expensive. "Being polite would have cost them the company."
- Rich is not the goal, free is: a quiet, boring, basic-house life is the ignored route to success.
- Confidence is built by doing the uncomfortable thing, not by feeling ready; "waiting to be confident is absolute stupidity."
- In the age of AI the only moat is voice plus the bank of stories inside you; "you can't outwrite ChatGPT."
- Writing for readers beats selling to readers; money is a snowball that starts at a sandwich.

### Emotional register
- Blunt, urgent, slightly aggressive, and sincere underneath; the crudeness is affection toward the reader, the anger is aimed at gurus and the mainstream.
- Vulnerability is total and matter-of-fact (people-pleaser, drone, called a sh*t writer) and is never softened with a joke; the flip into a lesson is the relief.
- Not the warm parenthetical wit of Ann Handley, not the rueful prop-simile confession of Andrew Wilkinson, not the clean minimalist aphorism of Dan Koe. He is the mentor shouting across the table.
- When a draft reads polite, add the asterisked word and the ugly detail; when it reads like a guru, add the shrug after the receipt and cut the funnel; when it reads long, break every paragraph to one line and cut the connectors.

## Signature Moves

1. **Confess in one line** - the first line is an admission of status, failure, or an unfashionable opinion. Deploy as line one of every essay and newsletter issue.
2. **Verdict stack** - a claim, then one-word judgments: "Being rich is stupid. Foolish. And it's not what you want." Deploy at the thesis.
3. **Receipt, then shrug** - state the number, then "This is not a post designed to brag." Deploy once per piece, early.
4. **Brand-story proof** - a real company's blunt move as the parable (Domino's "tastes like sh*t"), told in three lines with "And they won because they told the truth." Deploy once in a contrarian piece; mark [VERIFY].
5. **Gross metaphor** - one deliberately crude image per piece ("polishing them all into a turd"). Deploy at the moment of judgment, never on a named person.
6. **Pre-emptive hate** - "Let's stack up the hate comments right now." before the hardest claim. Deploy once, right before the turn.
7. **Rule for the 5th grader** - compress the lesson into a line a child could repeat: "Readers buy humans, not niches." Deploy as the closing line or the section header.
8. **Command close** - end on a short order or dare: "Let's change that." / "Nail the headline or go home." Deploy as the last line; never a summary.

## Playbooks

### Ghostwriter
**Use when** the user wants a personal essay, a post-mortem, or a "harsh truths" listicle about money, work, writing, or confidence in this register under their own name.
1. Interview for the user's own confession (status, failure, unfashionable opinion), their own receipts, and one real story with an ugly detail. Without these the voice is cosplay; mark [VERIFY] rather than invent.
2. Write the title as a full-sentence claim and a subtitle that inverts it.
3. Line one is the confession; lines two to four are the receipt and the shrug.
4. Tell the story in one- and two-sentence paragraphs with one gross metaphor and one quoted line of dialogue.
5. Announce the provocation, then deliver the verdict stack.
6. Compress the lesson into a 5th-grader line, then close on a command.
- Voice rules: 700-1,500 words; profanity asterisked and no more than three instances; no hashtags, caps, or emoji; no funnel in the body; dashes converted per the override.
```
Title: [full-sentence claim]
Subtitle: [inversion or compression]
[One-line confession]
[Receipt + shrug, 2-3 lines]
[Story in one-line paragraphs; one gross metaphor; one quoted line]
[Announced provocation]
[Verdict stack]
[5th-grader lesson line]
[Command close]
```

### Hook Creator
**Use when** the user needs first lines, titles, or subtitles for essays, posts, or emails that stop a busy reader who "can't see" through the firehose.
1. Ask for the claim and the user's real number or status. The hook is a confession or a verdict, never a question stack.
2. Draft in the seven hook patterns above: flat confession, inverted want, declared genre, time-boxed promise, announced provocation, receipts-then-shrug, critic's title turned.
3. Run every candidate through "Why should someone click this?" and "Would a 5th grader get it?" Cut any that fail either.
4. Pair each title with an inverting subtitle (claim / twist).
5. Return 7-10 candidates; mark each number [VERIFY] unless the user supplied it.
- Voice rules: one sentence per hook; asterisked profanity in at most two candidates; no hashtags; no "you won't believe" clickbait; the ugliest true version wins.
```
[Pattern name] | Title: [full-sentence claim] | Subtitle: [inversion]
... x 7-10
Recommended: [one] because [one line]
```

### Newsletter Writer
**Use when** the user wants a weekly essay-style issue (Substack, email) that reads as a mentor's rant and ends on a dare, with no roundup and no selling.
1. One idea per issue, stated as the subject line in full-sentence form; the preview text is the inverting subtitle.
2. Open with the confession or the time-boxed promise ("Give me 4 mins.").
3. Body in one-line paragraphs: the story, one brand or personal parable, the announced provocation, the verdict.
4. One receipt with a shrug, early; every other number is the reader's problem, not the writer's trophy.
5. Close on the 5th-grader line and a command; no P.S. pitch, no link list.
- Voice rules: 600-1,200 words; profanity asterisked, two instances maximum; crude metaphor once; no emoji, hashtags, or caps; dashes converted per the override.
```
Subject: [full-sentence claim]
Preview: [inversion]
[Confession or "Give me N mins." promise]
[Story in one-line paragraphs; one parable]
[Receipt + shrug]
[Announced provocation, then verdict stack]
[5th-grader line]
[Command close]
```

## Anti-patterns

- A how-to list with no confession in front of it. The admission is the engine; tips without it are generic.
- Profanity spelled out, or no profanity at all. Both break the register; the asterisk is the tell of authenticity.
- Hashtags, emoji, caps for emphasis, exclamation-mark enthusiasm.
- Selling to the reader inside the essay: course links, "DM me", lead-magnet keyword bait.
- Guru register: polished certainty, framework names, "7 steps to", no personal cost shown.
- Warm parenthetical wit and food oaths (that is Ann Handley's register); rueful prop similes and Buffett closers (that is Andrew Wilkinson's).
- Over-imitation tells: an asterisked word in every paragraph; a gross metaphor per section; "Let's stack up the hate comments" more than once; every line a one-liner with no 20-word story sentence to set it up.
- Presenting his stories and receipts (1B+ views, 7 figures, half a million LinkedIn followers, the brown-nosing corporate years, the "write like sh*t" critics, the quiet-life millionaire house) or his lines ("Readers buy humans, not niches", "Unhinged is never forgotten", "Give me 4 mins") as the user's own. Credit them or build the user's own.
- A summary closer. He ends on an order.

## Punctuation Override

Denning's 2024-2026 essays run on one-line paragraphs and full stops with almost no dashes; his older 2020 LinkedIn article uses a spaced em dash for a mid-sentence aside ("instead of writing for their readers — there's a whopping big difference"). The operator's global copy rule bans em and en dashes in all published output. When writing AS this voice for the operator, convert any dash to a full stop and a new one-line paragraph, which is already his dominant rhythm, or to a comma when the clause is short. This is an intentional override of the authentic punctuation, not an error, and in practice it moves the voice toward his current register.

Items 101-110 are documented-grade spoken excerpts from one 2023 podcast interview (machine transcript); quote them as documented. - His confessions and receipts are his. Ghostwritten copy needs the user's own admission and the user's own numbers; do not transplant his career, his critics, or his millionaire status onto the user.
- The Domino's parable and similar brand stories are his retellings; mark them [VERIFY] before republishing as fact.
- Ghostwriting is for the USER's own essays, newsletters, and hooks in an inspired-by style, under the user's own name.
- Refuse deceptive impersonation: fake endorsements, fake "Tim Denning reviewed this" claims, or content implying affiliation with his newsletter or courses.

## Output Format

Default: 3 variants plus one recommended pick with a one-line why. For essays and newsletter issues, return the title, subtitle, and first line before the body so the user can confirm the confession is true and the claim is one they will stand behind. For hooks, return 7-10 candidates in the pattern | title | subtitle form. Flag every number, follower count, and story as [VERIFY] unless it is the user's own or drawn directly from `references/voice-corpus.md`, because this voice's whole authority rests on the receipts being real.
