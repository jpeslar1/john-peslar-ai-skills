# Wes Kao Voice Corpus

Research date: 2026-10-19

The excerpts below are short quotations from public posts, articles, and talks, included under fair use for style analysis and attribution. They are third-party content and NOT covered by this repository's MIT license.

**Provenance note.** All 34 items are verbatim written text copied from raw fetched sources on the research date. Items 1-24 are from Wes Kao's Newsletter on Substack (2023-2026), pulled through the Substack posts API with full bodies; every issue checked is free to all readers, and several are her canonical essays re-hosted from her earlier site and marked as expanded. Items 25-30 are her own LinkedIn posts (2020-2023), read from logged-out permalinks located by search and from activity links embedded in her newsletter HTML; item 30 quotes four of seven contiguous bullets. Items 31-34 are from her 2020 essay on weskao.com, the one Squarespace-era post still directly fetchable. Typographic apostrophes and quotation marks were normalized to straight ASCII; wording, CAPS, asterisks, and the no-sign and check-mark labels are otherwise untouched (item 3 keeps a stray closing quotation mark from the source). Nothing here comes from a quote aggregator. There are no documented-grade items: her podcast appearances were paywalled or not transcribed, her 2024-2026 LinkedIn posts were not sampled, and she has no book. Items 101-110 are a separate documented-grade spoken sample from her 2025 Lenny's Podcast interview, machine-transcribed with speaker diarization from the free RSS audio (the page transcript is paywalled).

**Punctuation note.** Item 33 preserves an em dash because it is a verbatim quotation; it is the only dash in the sample, which otherwise runs on CAPS, asterisks, and short sentences. The operator's copy rule still bans em and en dashes in anything generated in this voice; see the Punctuation Override in SKILL.md.

## Derived stats (honest estimates from this corpus)

- Median sentence length: ~15 words in essays, with frequent 5-8 word definitional sentences ("Backstory scope creep is real.", "An insight is just a start."); LinkedIn lines run ~12 words.
- One-line paragraphs: roughly 25% of essay paragraphs are a single sentence, usually the thesis or the rule; on LinkedIn about 60% of lines stand alone.
- Hook-type frequency across the 34 items: named concept or flat distinction ~30%, two-operator or two-marketer contrast ~15%, "Let's say..." hypothetical ~15%, script pair (no-sign line then check-mark line) ~15%, short anecdote cut to its climax ~10%, spiky claim with its reason ~15%.
- Script pairs and quoted example sentences: present in roughly a third of items; the good version is always a complete, pasteable sentence ("I'd like to create an automated table... This should take half a day to build. How does that sound?").
- Rhetorical questions: about one per essay section, always answered or left as the reader's homework ("Which operator do you think adds more value?", "What do you think I mean?").
- CAPS for emphasis: one word per piece at most (MUST, THIS, WHAT??); asterisks for softer emphasis (*make it intentional*, *strategic reframe*) about as often.
- Emoji: only the no-sign and check-mark labels on script pairs, a cactus in one essay title, and a waving hand in her standard header; none as decoration in sentences; hashtags: on 2020 LinkedIn posts only.
- Profanity and casual drops: "wtf" once, "god forbid" once, "y'all" once, "stuff" and "dumb" inside quoted bad examples; otherwise plain register.
- Hedges and scoping: explicit pre-emption ("I can already hear folks saying", "To be clear") and scoped claims ("usually the right amount", "not as thoughtful as it seems") in roughly a third of items; no vague intensifiers.
- Numbers: small and concrete; half a day, 1,500 versus 150 words, 90% trimmed, 500 then 50 words, two weeks, 8am ET, 15 principles; no percentages from studies.
- Favorite openers: the concept name as title; "There is a difference between X vs Y"; "Let's say I send you..."; "Operator #1 says... Operator #2 says..."; a rule stated flat ("Rigorous thinking is where flimsy ideas go to die.").
- Favorite closers: the rule restated as one line ("Start your story right before you get eaten by the bear."), a "Do this today" action block, and the standing sign-off naming the next issue's day and time; on LinkedIn a repost ask.
- Spoken vs written (items 101-110, one 2025 podcast interview): spoken sentences run 25-45 words against ~15 in essays, but the essay moves carry over: a named concept ('blast radius', 'take the win'), a pet-peeve or flat-dislike opener ('One of my pet peeves is...', 'I really hate excessive formatting.'), and a paired restatement ('you can't cut to the chase unless you know what the chase is'). 'I'm a big proponent of' recurs as her spoken stance marker (items 101, 110); fillers ('uh', 'um', 'you know', 'like') sit in most answers and 'right?' tags close claims; one 'oh shit' (item 106) against a near-clean written register.
