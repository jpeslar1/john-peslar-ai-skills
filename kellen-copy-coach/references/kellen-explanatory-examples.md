# Kellen explanatory examples and psychology map

Use this reference when the user wants deeper feedback, Kellen-style psychology, or a critique that explains why the copy works or fails. These patterns are distilled from outbound copy feedback patterns from Kellen Casebeer of The Deal Lab (https://thedeallab.com/).

Do not quote large blocks back to the user. Convert these into concise critique, rules, and rewrites.

## 1. Style taxonomy: offer-centric, Will-style brevity, Josh-inspired agitation

Kellen distinguishes between several valid outbound styles:

- Offer-centric: first line is the offer, second line is context, often around 50 words.
- Will Allred-style: 75 words or less, simple, clean, more structured, less transactional.
- Josh Braun-inspired: agitate the status quo, focus on “you” framing, reduce sender-centric language.
- Audience-led iteration: run a breadth of angles, then zero in based on what the audience rewards.

How to use it:

- Do not treat one copy style as universally correct.
- Ask what style the user is attempting: direct offer, soft intrigue, agitation, proof-led, permission-to-send, founder-note, etc.
- If the copy is not working, suggest a different style test, not just word edits.
- If the copy is heavy or confusing, default to a Will-style shorter version.
- If the copy is bland or too polite, default to a Josh-style status quo agitation version.

## 2. Square-one buyer psychology: personal motive vs company demand

Kellen’s recurring idea:

- People buy based on perceived pain, opportunity to gain, risk, and how much they personally care.
- The company has demands. The person has desires, pressure, status concerns, fear, ambition, and convenience needs.
- Strong outbound helps the individual get what they personally want while making it look like an organizational benefit.

Use this as the first psychological audit:

1. What does the company want?
2. What does the individual want?
3. What would make the individual look smart, safe, fast, strategic, or in control?
4. How can the email frame that personal win as a company win?

Examples:

- CX leader hiring reps: company wants coverage; individual wants less ramp, less management burden, less weekend/PTO chaos.
- Agency owner: company wants pipeline; founder wants not to be trapped in founder-led sales or forced to hire SDRs too early.
- AI founder: company wants revenue; founder wants pipeline without losing product/strategy attention.

## 3. Empathy is underrated, but not soft

Kellen praises “self awareness and chippy empathy,” not apologetic softness.

Operating rule:

- Empathy should make the buyer feel seen, not coddled.
- The tone can be self-aware, slightly cheeky, or “shit eating grin” confident.
- Do not confuse empathy with over-politeness.
- Do not confuse personalization with empathy.

Good empathy identifies the buyer’s real tension:

- “Founder-led sales usually works until it starts costing too much founder attention.”
- “Volume is rising faster than training, weekends, and PTO can comfortably absorb.”
- “Agency referrals can create momentum, but they rarely make pipeline feel predictable.”

Bad empathy:

- “I know you’re busy.”
- “Love your work, you’re crushing it.”
- “Just wanted to reach out.”

## 4. Personalization is optional; relevance is not

Kellen explicitly pushes back against overrating 1:1 personalization:

- People buy non-personalized products every day.
- Personalization only matters if it strengthens relevance, empathy, or the argument.
- Presumed personalization can work better than “noticed X.”

Presumed personalization:

- Instead of “noticed you’ve been CEO for X years,” subtly land that knowledge while making the argument.
- The focus should remain on the claim, not the fact that the sender did research.

Rule:

- If the personalized line ends with “noticed X,” check whether X matters.
- If X only proves effort, cut it.
- If X changes the business tension, keep it and make it serve the argument.

## 5. Complex deals need simpler mechanisms

Kellen’s critique of complex offers:

- Complex deals often produce emails that are too complex.
- Break the full solution into a simple mechanism.
- Focus less on “the full solution” and more on “why they’d say yes.”
- Selling the meeting is not the same as selling the purchase.

Key distinction:

- “Why people buy” is often too far downstream for a cold email.
- “Why it is worth talking” can be much simpler.
- More of the TAM has intrigue or curiosity than active buying intent.

Use this for complex B2B:

1. Identify one narrow mechanism.
2. Tie it to one buyer pain or curiosity.
3. Make the CTA about learning, comparing, or seeing if the idea is true.
4. Do not pitch the whole platform.

Example:

- Bad: “We solve your GTM motion with AI-powered automation, enrichment, orchestration, and analytics.”
- Better: “Teams usually miss follow-ups when deals depend on three handoffs. I had an idea for where that may be happening at {{company}}.”

## 6. The “why they’d talk” test

Before rewriting, ask:

- Would this make someone curious enough to talk even if they are not ready to buy?
- Does it give them a useful idea, wedge, checklist, comparison, sample, teardown, report, or angle?
- Is the call framed as a learning opportunity or as a vendor pitch?

Prefer CTAs like:

- “Would it help to see the first wedge we’d test?”
- “Would it be useful to see the targeting angle?”
- “Would it help to compare that path against hiring inside {{company}}?”
- “Can I send the checklist?”
- “If I map how I’d approach this, will you take a look?”

Avoid defaulting to:

- “Worth a chat?”
- “Worth 10 minutes?”
- “Open to a quick call?”

Those are not always wrong, but they are usually too generic for skeptical first touch.

## 7. Status quo disruption plus fairness

Kellen’s Abhi feedback says the first goal is to disrupt status quo, then balance agitation with empathy.

Use this formula:

1. Name the status quo.
2. Show why it might not be enough.
3. Keep the tone fair.
4. Make the next step specific and useful.

Good agitation:

- “You won’t close that gap by putting out another random blog post.”
- “A scanner report + logo used to pass - not anymore.”
- “Founder-led sales usually works until it starts costing too much founder attention.”

Bad agitation:

- insults the buyer
- uses fake certainty
- makes the sender sound arrogant without earning it
- creates defensiveness but no useful next step

## 8. Sincere scarcity and anti-gotcha framing

Kellen likes sincere scarcity when it is real. The danger is making the recipient look for the hole.

Rules:

- If there is a legitimate limitation, say it plainly.
- Do not perform “this is exclusive” energy unless the reason is obvious.
- Make the reason for targeting and the sender benefit clear enough that the offer feels honest.
- Do not make scarcity feel like a trick.

Useful structure:

- “We’re partnering with X.”
- “They asked if there are good people to connect with ahead of time.”
- “The room is small because Y.”
- “Do you mind if I intro you?”

The psychology:

- When scarcity feels sincere, it reduces suspicion.
- When scarcity feels like bait, the recipient searches for the lie.

## 9. First line must stand alone

Kellen frequently flags openers that only make sense after line two.

Audit:

- If line one creates an open loop, does it answer “what / where / why this person” clearly enough?
- Would the buyer understand the email if they only read line one?
- Is the line buyer-centric, or does it make the sender/event/company the hero?

Fix:

- Put the where, what, and buyer-specific context in line one.
- Replace “joining us?” with “are you planning to be at X in Y?”
- Replace generic praise with the business tension.

## 10. Cognitive load: the hidden killer

Kellen’s Matt July 4 feedback is a clean model:

- The original was too long.
- It stacked too many claims.
- That increased cognitive load.
- The strongest idea was not the pricing mechanic.
- The strongest idea was the operational tension.

Audit for cognitive load:

- How many claims are being made?
- How many proof points?
- How many mechanisms?
- How many CTAs?
- How much does the buyer need to infer?
- Is the best idea buried under details?

Fix:

- Pick the strongest tension.
- Cut distracting PS lines.
- Use proof in follow-up if the first touch is already heavy.
- Replace “all the reasons we’re credible” with “one reason this problem matters.”

## 11. Proof relevance beats proof volume

Proof only works when the buyer can map it to their situation.

Common failures:

- Proof from a publisher used for an agency prospect.
- “Similar company” proof that is too vague.
- Customer counts plus awards plus metrics stacked before the problem is clear.
- Big sending volume proof for AI founders, which may imply spam risk.

Rules:

- If proof creates a relevance gap, cut it or move it later.
- Use one quiet credibility line.
- If the proof is weak, make the ask softer.
- Proof should make the buyer think, “that sounds like my situation,” not “cool story.”

## 12. Guarantee skepticism

Kellen often sees guarantees as doing too much work too early.

Why guarantees backfire:

- The buyer has heard similar claims.
- The guarantee screams commodity vendor or appointment setter.
- The stronger the guarantee, the more skeptical the reader becomes.
- Free meetings or performance-only offers can make the service feel cheap.

Use guarantees later unless:

- the market expects them,
- the guarantee is very believable,
- the offer is transactional enough that the guarantee reduces friction more than it raises doubt.

For first touch, prefer:

- softer language
- test/wedge/checklist/sample
- proof-relevant comparison
- curiosity-based CTA

## 13. Motivation, targeting, skepticism, proof, CTA lens

Kellen’s Ali teardown can be reused as a formal diagnostic.

### Motivation lens

Functional benefits are not enough. Ask what strategic or emotional pressure matters:

- inconsistent pipeline
- founder-led sales bottleneck
- feast-or-famine months
- avoiding hires before the motion is proven
- not distracting senior people from delivery

### Targeting lens

Firmographics are not a sharp angle by themselves.

Weak:

- “digital marketing agencies, 11 to 50 employees”

Sharper:

- “agencies that need more sales conversations without adding junior outbound hires or distracting senior people from delivery”

### Skepticism lens

Big claims should be softened, not amplified.

### Proof lens

Proof must match the prospect’s world.

### CTA lens

The ask should match readiness.

Meeting asks require more belief. Permission asks can create the first reply.

## 14. The “wrong audience” diagnosis

If the copy sounds written for another market, say so directly.

Examples:

- “This sounds like agencies with retainer churn, not B2B AI founders.”
- “This reads like a lead-gen vendor email, not an agency-owner email.”
- “This proof maps to a publisher, not the agency buyer.”

Then rewrite around:

- actual business model
- actual buyer risk
- actual buying pressure
- actual internal incentive

## 15. Give-away strategy when proof is thin

For local/service offers where proof is weak, Kellen sometimes pushes toward doing the thing manually or free first instead of scaling cold email prematurely.

Pattern:

- “Why scaled email vs walking in/local first?”
- “Can I give you a high-value service for free so I can sell it to the next 500 outside your customer range?”

Use this when:

- the user has no results yet
- the offer is local
- the service is visual or demonstrable
- the fastest path is a lighthouse case, not immediate scale

Skill behavior:

- Do not only rewrite the email.
- Tell the user when the strategy should change from scaled outbound to proof acquisition.

## 16. List, infra, and offer desire are valid diagnoses

If the copy seems good, Kellen may point away from copy.

Possible diagnoses:

- volume
- inboxing/infra
- list quality
- targeting
- market simply does not want the offer

Skill behavior:

- If the copy is strong, say so.
- Suggest likely non-copy bottlenecks.
- Do not over-edit good copy to feel useful.

## 17. Softening language

Kellen sometimes softens certainty:

- “seems like”
- “feels like”
- “might mean”
- “usually”
- “from what we see”

Use softening when:

- the sender cannot prove the claim
- the line risks sounding arrogant
- the prospect may disagree
- the email needs to lower defensiveness

Do not soften the core tension so much that it loses force.

## 18. “We” lines and I:you ratio

Kellen’s Josh Braun reference:

- Watch I:you ratio.
- Focus on “you” framing.
- Remove “we” lines that add nothing.
- The buyer usually does not care about the sender yet.

But do not mechanically delete all “we” lines. Keep “we” when it clarifies the mechanism after buyer relevance is established.

Good sequence:

1. Buyer situation.
2. Consequence or tension.
3. Sender mechanism in one line.
4. Soft CTA.

## 19. Subject lines: specific term + gap

For search-demand or gap emails, Kellen favors concrete subject lines:

- “{search term} gap”
- “gtm revops gap”

Principle:

- Use the buyer’s exact small term when possible.
- Lowercase can feel more natural.
- Avoid vague “search gap” if a more specific keyword is available.

## 20. CTA should often be one sentence

Kellen repeatedly cuts CTAs down.

Rules:

- One sentence.
- Specific loop.
- Low friction.
- Match the thing you are offering.

Examples:

- “Would it help to see the angle we’d test?”
- “Can I send a sample?”
- “Would it help to compare that against hiring in-house?”

## 21. Run variants by conceptual angle, not synonym swaps

When suggesting A/B/C tests:

- vary brevity
- vary angle
- vary proof placement
- vary CTA type
- vary paragraph architecture
- vary whether the opener is offer-first or tension-first

Do not just paraphrase the same email three ways.

Useful split:

- A: educational + rapport
- B: educational but direct
- C: direct and concise

## 22. Critique templates

### Blunt first pass

Verdict: closest version is [X], but it is still [too long / too sender-centric / too proof-heavy].

Core issue: [one sentence].

Psychology: [buyer is not ready for the claim/meeting; they need a reason to be curious].

Rewrite: [brief copy].

### Deep teardown

Use the five-lens structure:

1. Motivation lens
2. Targeting lens
3. Skepticism lens
4. Proof lens
5. CTA lens

Then write 3-5 variants.

### Strategy correction

If the channel/strategy is wrong:

- “I’d question whether this should be scaled email yet.”
- “You may need a lighthouse case first.”
- “This might be a list/infra/offer-desire issue, not a copy issue.”

Then give the best next test.

## 23. Example output style

Keep the voice direct:

- “This reads like a vendor email.”
- “The insight is good, but you’re trying to prove too much.”
- “Cut the proof pile.”
- “The CTA is too heavy for the level of belief you’ve created.”
- “Lead with the real pain.”
- “Pick one tension only.”
- “The shorter version is the one I’d test first.”

Then show the rewrite.
