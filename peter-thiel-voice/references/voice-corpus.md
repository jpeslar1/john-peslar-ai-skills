# Peter Thiel Voice Corpus

Research date: 2026-10-18

The excerpts below are short quotations from public essays, book excerpts, class notes, and one talk, included under fair use for style analysis and attribution. They are third-party content and NOT covered by this repository's MIT license.

**Provenance note.** Items 1-28 are verbatim: copied character for character from the live page, a publisher PDF mirror, or a Wayback Machine capture of the original article (National Review and WSJ, whose live pages are bot-walled or paywalled). Items 29-30 are DOCUMENTED, not verbatim: they come from Blake Masters' student notes of Thiel's Stanford CS183 lectures, which Masters himself flags ("Errors and omissions are my own"). Items 31-33 are DOCUMENTED: YouTube auto-captions (ASR) of his Cambridge Union talk, with filler words dropped and no words changed; the source has no punctuation. Nothing here comes from a quote aggregator. Thiel has no active social channel, so there is no short-form section. Update 2026-10-19: items 101-110 add an interview-register spoken sample, documented-grade diarized ASR of one 2024 Uncommon Knowledge episode, in the section "Spoken excerpts" below.

## Verbatim excerpts

1. "I remain committed to the faith of my teenage years: to authentic human freedom as a precondition for the highest good. I stand against confiscatory taxes, totalitarian collectives, and the ideology of the inevitability of the death of every individual. For all these reasons, I still call myself "libertarian.""
    The Education of a Libertarian, Cato Unbound, 2009-04-13, https://www.cato-unbound.org/2009/04/13/peter-thiel/education-libertarian/ , verbatim

2. "But I must confess that over the last two decades, I have changed radically on the question of how to achieve these goals. Most importantly, I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible."
    The Education of a Libertarian, Cato Unbound, 2009-04-13, same URL, verbatim

3. "Much of it felt like trench warfare on the Western Front in World War I; there was a lot of carnage, but we did not move the center of the debate. In hindsight, we were preaching mainly to the choir — even if this had the important side benefit of convincing the choir's members to continue singing for the rest of their lives."
    The Education of a Libertarian, Cato Unbound, 2009-04-13, same URL, verbatim

4. "The higher one's IQ, the more pessimistic one became about free-market politics — capitalism simply is not that popular with the crowd. Among the smartest conservatives, this pessimism often manifested in heroic drinking; the smartest libertarians, by contrast, had fewer hang-ups about positive law and escaped not only to alcohol but beyond it."
    The Education of a Libertarian, Cato Unbound, 2009-04-13, same URL, verbatim

5. "A better metaphor is that we are in a deadly race between politics and technology. The future will be much better or much worse, but the question of the future remains very open indeed. We do not know exactly how close this race is, but I suspect that it may be very close, even down to the wire."
    The Education of a Libertarian, Cato Unbound, 2009-04-13, same URL, verbatim

6. "I believe that politics is way too intense. That's why I'm a libertarian. Politics gets people angry, destroys relationships, and polarizes peoples' vision: the world is us versus them; good people versus the other. Politics is about interfering with other people's lives without their consent."
    The Education of a Libertarian (editor-appended follow-up remarks), Cato Unbound, 2009-05, same URL, verbatim

7. "For the rationalists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, as well as for all those who consider themselves cosmopolitan today, this sort of hysterical talk about the end of the world was deemed to be the exclusive province of people who were either stupid or wicked or insane (although mostly just stupid)."
    The Optimistic Thought Experiment, Policy Review / Hoover Institution, 2008-01-29, https://www.hoover.org/research/optimistic-thought-experiment , verbatim

8. "And yet, if the truth were to be told, our slumber is not as peaceful as it once was. Beginning with the Great War in 1914, and accelerating after 1945, there has re-emerged an apocalyptic dimension to the modern world. In a strange way, however, this apocalyptic dimension has arisen from the very place that was meant to liberate us from antediluvian fears."
    The Optimistic Thought Experiment, Policy Review / Hoover Institution, 2008-01-29, same URL, verbatim

9. "The news and business sections seem to inhabit different worlds that coexist on the same planet but rarely intersect. Most financial actors are content to rule their separate kingdom, and to refrain from unprofitable questions about the integrity of the larger whole."
    The Optimistic Thought Experiment, Policy Review / Hoover Institution, 2008-01-29, same URL, verbatim

10. "The twenty-first century started with a bang on September 11, 2001. In those shocking hours, the entire political and military framework of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and indeed of the modern age, with its emphasis on deterrent armies, rational nation-states, public debates, and international diplomacy, was called into question."
    The Straussian Moment, in *Politics and Apocalypse* (Michigan State UP), 2007, https://gwern.net/doc/philosophy/religion/2007-thiel-thestraussianmoment.pdf , verbatim

11. "From the Enlightenment on, modern political philosophy has been characterized by the abandonment of a set of questions that an earlier age had deemed central: What is a well-lived life? What does it mean to be human? What is the nature of the city and humanity? How does culture and religion fit into all of this? For the modern world, the death of God was followed by the disappearance of the question of human nature."
    The Straussian Moment, 2007, same URL, verbatim

12. "Indeed, there is little in Strauss that is more clear than the need for less transparency."
    The Straussian Moment, 2007, same URL, verbatim

13. "And so, in determining the correct mixture of violence and peace, the Christian statesman or stateswoman would be wise, in every close case, to side with peace. There is no formula to answer the critical question of what constitutes a "close case"; that must be decided in every specific instance."
    The Straussian Moment, 2007, same URL, verbatim

14. "Modern Western civilization stands on the twin plinths of science and technology. Taken together, these two interrelated domains reassure us that the 19th-century story of never-ending progress remains intact."
    The End of the Future, National Review, 2011-10-03, https://www.nationalreview.com/2011/10/end-future-peter-thiel/ (read via Wayback), verbatim

15. "In our hearts and minds, we know that desperate optimism will not save us. Progress is neither automatic nor mechanistic; it is rare."
    The End of the Future, National Review, 2011-10-03, same URL, verbatim

16. "Consider the most literal instance of non-acceleration: We are no longer moving faster."
    The End of the Future, National Review, 2011-10-03, same URL, verbatim

17. ""Clean tech" has become a euphemism for "energy too expensive to afford," and in Silicon Valley it has also become an increasingly toxic term for near-certain ways to lose money."
    The End of the Future, National Review, 2011-10-03, same URL, verbatim

18. "Men reached the moon in July 1969, and Woodstock began three weeks later. With the benefit of hindsight, we can see that this was when the hippies took over the country, and when the true cultural war over Progress was lost."
    The End of the Future, National Review, 2011-10-03, same URL, verbatim

19. "What valuable company is nobody building? This question is harder than it looks, because your company could create a lot of value without becoming very valuable itself. Creating value isn't enough—you also need to capture some of the value you create."
    Competition Is for Losers, WSJ (adapted from *Zero to One*), 2014-09-12, https://www.wsj.com/articles/peter-thiel-competition-is-for-losers-1410535536 (read via Wayback), verbatim

20. "Monopolists lie to protect themselves. They know that bragging about their great monopoly invites being audited, scrutinized and attacked. Since they very much want their monopoly profits to continue unmolested, they tend to do whatever they can to conceal their monopoly—usually by exaggerating the power of their (nonexistent) competition."
    Competition Is for Losers, WSJ, 2014-09-12, same URL, verbatim

21. "Tolstoy famously opens "Anna Karenina" by observing: "All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." Business is the opposite. All happy companies are different: Each one earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem. All failed companies are the same: They failed to escape competition."
    Competition Is for Losers, WSJ, 2014-09-12, same URL, verbatim

22. "Technology means doing more with less. In the absence of technological progress, we end up with a zero-sum world, in which there must be a loser for every winner."
    Against Edenism, First Things, 2015-06-01, https://www.firstthings.com/article/2015/06/against-edenism , verbatim

23. "Just about every science-fiction movie of the past quarter century portrays science and technology as a trap that humanity is building for itself. One may choose from a menu of dystopias, from The Terminator to The Matrix to Elysium to Avatar. A film in which a Luddite, an environmental extremist, or an FDA regulator is the arch-villain does not get made; as it so often does, Hollywood both creates and reflects the broader cultural consensus."
    Against Edenism, First Things, 2015-06-01, same URL, verbatim

24. "I badly miss the misguided optimism of a Faust—at least he was motivated to try to do something about everything that was wrong with the world."
    Against Edenism, First Things, 2015-06-01, same URL, verbatim

25. "When Boeing introduced its flagship 707 jet airliner in 1958, the power to cruise at 977 kilometers per hour did more than enable routine transcontinental commercial flights. It fed the optimistic self-understanding of a society proud to have entered the Jet Age. More than sixty years later, we are not moving any faster."
    Back to the Future, First Things, 2020-03-01, https://www.firstthings.com/web-exclusives/2020/03/back-to-the-future , verbatim

26. "As for the world outside of MIT's PR materials, it appears much the same as it was in 1969—just with faster computers and uglier cars."
    Back to the Future, First Things, 2020-03-01, same URL, verbatim

27. "It is a paradox of our time that the path to radical progress begins with moderation. Extreme optimism and fatalistic pessimism may seem to be stark opposites, but they both end in apathy. If things were sure to improve or bound to collapse, then our actions would not matter one way or the other."
    Back to the Future, First Things, 2020-03-01, same URL, verbatim

28. "Warp drive is in fact hard to take seriously because its basic physics are so far beyond the furthest reaches of our knowledge as to debilitate would-be researchers—not to mention reasonable doubts about the friendliness of faraway foreign species. (Some of them, I assume, are good aliens.)"
    Back to the Future, First Things, 2020-03-01, same URL, verbatim

## Derived stats (honest estimates from this corpus)

- Median sentence length: ~28 words in essays, with periodic sentences reaching 50-60 words; the WSJ book excerpt drops to ~17.
- One-line paragraphs: effectively none; paragraphs run 4-8 sentences.
- Hook-type frequency across the seven essays: civilizational opener with dated specific ~45%, inverted commonplace ~25%, epigraph ~15%, valuable question ~15%.
- Parenthetical asides: 1-2 per essay carry the humor; none are signposted.
- Direct reader address: zero in the six philosophical and political essays; "your company" appears only in the WSJ/Zero to One excerpt.
- Exclamation marks: zero across ~30,000 words sampled.
- Profanity: zero.
- Numbers: dates, years, speeds, and named events in nearly every paragraph; percentages rare.
- Canonical anchors per essay: 2-5 named thinkers or texts (Strauss, Schmitt, Girard, Tolstoy, Bacon, Hobbes, Nietzsche, Malthus, Luke, Revelation).
- Favorite openers: a dated event at civilizational scale; a fair restatement of a consensus about to be inverted.
- Favorite closers: a short declarative sentence that restates the inversion or names the open future ("the question of the future remains very open indeed").
- Spoken register (ASR sample): same thesis-shaped sentences padded with "sort of," "in some ways," "you know," "somehow"; restarts common; no change in stance or vocabulary.
- Spoken vs written, interview register (items 101-110, one 40-minute Uncommon Knowledge interview, 2024): answers open on 'Well,' and a meta-comment on the question ('that's very overdetermined', used twice in 40 minutes, items 102 and 107) before the thesis arrives. Sentences run as long as the essays (30-60 words) but are chained with 'and' and 'and then' instead of semicolons, with heavy 'um'/'uh' and doubled words ('I'm not, I'm not'). The parenthetical joke of the essays becomes a deadpan aside delivered at the same pitch ('trapped for 3, 3 hours', 'dead of the world unite', 'playing Pong'). He tags conclusions with 'or something like this' and steelmans opponents before demoting them ('the effective altruists are winning the argument', the Fauci protocol). Hedges ('I think', 'probably', 'maybe') are far more frequent than in writing. Profanity and exclamations: zero.
