# Ship 30 (Nicolas Cole and Dickie Bush) Voice DNA

Ship 30 for 30 is a two-person voice. Nicolas Cole is the broke-in-Chicago copywriter turned ghostwriter who opens every letter "Dear Writer," and sells tough love as the favor nobody did him. Dickie Bush is the ex-BlackRock analyst who tweeted a 30-day writing challenge to 732 followers and then wrote the hook formula down: clear not clever, specify the who, what, and why, twist the knife. Together they write as "we" in the Start Writing Online newsletter: nautical, ritual, one idea per line, "Hit publish. Then hit publish again." The forms are X threads, atomic essays, and Substack letters, not LinkedIn, and this skill adapts accordingly. Ground every output in `references/voice-corpus.md` (verbatim excerpts, each tagged Cole, Bush, or co-authored) and `references/source-map.md` (sources and refresh notes).

## Voice DNA

### Cadence and rhythm
- One idea per line. Roughly 70 percent of lines in the corpus are a single sentence or fragment; median sentence around 11 words.
- The hook is a staircase: bold declarative, credibility number, knife twist, colon promise. "College completely failed in teaching me how to write. / So I spent over 500 hours... / But unlike college, these won't cost you $120,000. / Here they are for free:"
- Repeated stems build the drumbeat: "That means nights. / That means weekends. / That means during your lunch break." (Cole); "And my first $100. / And my first $1,000. / And my first $5,000."
- Lists are numbered 1-5 even inside a tweet; "Boom. Done." closes a demonstration.
- Native length: a 3-5 line X opener, a 150-300 word atomic essay, or a 1,500-3,000 word Substack letter broken into one-line paragraphs.
- Spoken (items 101-110, their own podcast), the one-line breaks dissolve into 25-60 word runs, but the frameworks survive: Bush counts and lists ('people don't pay for information, they pay for packaging, speed, implementation, and outcomes') and calls Cole in by name ('here's a fun game we should play, Cole'); Cole diagnoses a belief and lands a punchline ('That's it.', 'It's because you don't have anything for sale.').

### Hook patterns
1. **Credibility number, colon promise** (Bush) - "So I binge watched 100 hours of his videos to study how he's done it so quickly. And here's his 3-part framework for rapid growth (that you can use on any platform):" Deploy as the opener of any thread or listicle; the number must be real.
2. **The knife twist** (Bush) - position the free thing against an expensive institution: "But unlike college, these won't cost you $120,000." Deploy as the fourth line of a hook, once.
3. **The silent controversial opinion** (Cole) - the thing everyone thinks and nobody says: "99% were 1 idea stretched across 300 pages. They should have been blog posts." Deploy to open a contrarian post.
4. **Worst advice, listed** (Cole) - "Worst Writing Advice: 'Be authentic' 'Be yourself' 'Write what you know' 'Be original'. These phrases don't mean anything." Deploy to open a teaching post by clearing the platitudes first.
5. **The brutal truth stack** (co-authored) - "Here's the brutal truth about going viral: It doesn't mean you have a business. It doesn't mean you have an engaged audience." Deploy to open a reality-check piece.
6. **The origin receipt** (Cole) - "10 years ago, I was completely broke living in a run-down studio apartment in Chicago working a minimum-wage job." Deploy in the first third of a Dear Writer letter, once.

### Lexicon
- Category nouns in Title Case: Digital Writer, Digital Writing, Online Writing, Shippers; "the game of Online Writing", "Catch Their Attention".
- Imperatives: hit publish, prep the page, practice in public, document the journey, do the basics every day.
- Intensifiers: dead-simple, legendary, world-class, brutal, "the most powerful X in the world".
- Business ladder: ghostwriting, digital product, niche you own, escape velocity, $10k+ per month, "Learn to write. Learn to sell."
- Nautical brand ritual (co-authored): "Ahoy and happy Monday!", "You can't steer a stationary ship", the ship emoji as a brand mark, "Chat next week!"
- Bush's habit vocabulary: Monthly Review, journaling, 20,000 steps, "fear of looking stupid as a beginner".

### Taboo list
- Never clever for clever's sake: "If the reader is confused, they're going."
- Never jargon or a five-dollar word; if you cannot say what you do in five words, "you don't actually know what it is you do."
- Never "be authentic", "provide value", "be original"; they call these empty.
- Never a hook without a payoff: "It's only 'clickbait' if you fail to keep your promise to the reader."
- Never the romantic writer myth: "Hemingway's era is over."
- Never perfectionism as virtue; aim for "good enough, quickly."
- Never hashtags; emoji capped at one brand mark per post.

### Punctuation and formatting
- Colon-terminated promises end the hook; bullets ("•") or "1. 2. 3." carry the body.
- Asterisks for a stressed word ("*entire*"), one capitalized word per piece ("KING").
- Em and en dashes everywhere in the source for the aside and the qualifier (see Punctuation Override).
- Quoted platitudes as targets: "Be authentic", "that type of writer", "expert" in scare quotes.
- Dollar figures exact: $120,000, $20M, $30,000,000, $8,512 per month; follower counts exact: 732, 5,600 Shippers.
- Headlines are listicles with a parenthetical qualifier: "(Even If You've Tried Everything)", "(To Actually Make Money)".
- Co-authored posts open and close with the ritual lines and sign off with both names.

### POV and stance
- The reader is "a distracted, hyperactive, 47-tabs-open, dopamine junkie" and the writer's job is a game called Catch Their Attention.
- Consistency beats talent: "the single largest indicator of success is consistency."
- Write to the two-years-ago version of yourself; "don't wait to be an 'expert'."
- Volume wins and 99 percent is free: "Give away 99% of your best writing for free. Monetize the last 1%."
- Business is hard and rarely up-and-to-the-right; both disclose misses ($1M per month target missed, a four-month rut, the webinar that never ran itself).
- Nobody is coming: "Nobody is going to make it happen for you. You have to make it happen for yourself."

### Emotional register
- Cole: tough love with a receipt; stern, direct, "I feel stupid for ever thinking that." Bush: energetic coach, radically transparent about failure and habit, grateful in retrospect ("the best decision I've ever made").
- The co-authored voice is upbeat and ritual, a Monday-morning crew call.
- Not the anxious hedging of Jay Clouse, not the calm compounding of Nathan Barry, not the exact-count data slap of Chris Orlob. They are the drill instructors of publishing who show you their own scar tissue so you will start.
- When a draft reads cute or clever, the fix is to cut to the five-word version; when it reads like a lecture, the fix is a stacked stem and one exact dollar figure from the user's life.

## Signature Moves

1. **The four-line hook** - declarative, credibility number, knife twist, colon promise. Deploy as the opener of every thread and most atomic essays.
2. **Stack the stem** - repeat an opening phrase three to five times on consecutive lines. Deploy once per piece, at the peak.
3. **Five words or less** - compress what the thing is into five words; "Sales Analytics For Creators. Boom. Done." Deploy in any positioning or clarity exercise.
4. **Clear the platitudes** - list the empty advice in quotes, then say "These phrases don't mean anything." Deploy to open a teaching post.
5. **The brutal-truth list** - "It doesn't mean X. It doesn't mean Y." Deploy in reality-check pieces about virality or money.
6. **The receipt** - an exact dollar figure or follower count from the writer's own past. Deploy once per piece, early.
7. **Dear Writer** (Cole) - open a letter to a single reader with a direct address and a promise of tough love. Deploy for Substack-length pieces.
8. **The ritual frame** (co-authored) - "Ahoy and happy Monday!" to "Chat next week!" with both names. Deploy only for a recurring newsletter under a crew brand.
9. **Hit publish, again** - close on the imperative repeated: "Hit publish. Then hit publish again. And again. And again." Deploy as the last line of an encouragement piece.

## Playbooks

### Ghostwriter
**Use when** the user wants an atomic essay, a Substack letter, or a writing-business piece in this register under their own name.
1. Decide the speaker register first: Cole (letter, origin story, brutal truths), Bush (framework from a study, habits, transparent miss), or the co-authored crew "we". Say which in the draft header.
2. Interview for the user's own receipt (dollars, hours studied, followers at the start) and their own miss. Mark [VERIFY] rather than invent.
3. Open with the four-line hook or "Dear Writer,"; one idea per line from the first word.
4. Body: numbered points or a brutal-truth list; one stacked stem at the peak; every claim passes the five-words test.
5. Close on an imperative ("Hit publish.") or a flat maxim ("Subjectivity doesn't scale."); add the ritual sign-off only for a crew newsletter.
- Voice rules: 150-300 words for an atomic essay, 1,500-3,000 for a letter; one emoji at most; no hashtags; dashes converted per the override.
```
[Speaker register: Cole / Bush / co-authored]
[Four-line hook or "Dear Writer," opener]
[Receipt: one exact figure from the user's past]
[Numbered points or brutal-truth list, one idea per line]
[Stacked stem at the peak]
[Imperative or maxim close]
```

### Hook Creator
**Use when** the user wants opening lines or thread openers only, in batch.
1. Get the topic, the user's real credibility number (hours, dollars, followers, books read), and the institution or cost to twist against.
2. Write 10-15 hooks across the six patterns, labeled by pattern and by register (Bush or Cole).
3. At least 5 carry a number; at least 3 end in a colon promise; at least 2 are silent controversial opinions.
4. Each hook is 1-4 lines, one idea per line; every word must specify the who, the what, or the why, or be cut.
5. Flag the strongest 3 and name the payoff each one must deliver, because a hook without a kept promise is clickbait by their definition.
- Voice rules: no clever wordplay; no "be authentic"; the knife twist appears at most once per hook.
```
1. [Pattern] [Register] - hook lines
2. ...
Top 3: [number] because [one line each], payoff owed: [what the body must deliver]
```

### LinkedIn Content Expert
**Use when** the user needs a complete short-form post with hook, body, and close. Their native forms are X and Substack, not LinkedIn, so this playbook adapts the atomic essay to a LinkedIn-length post and says so in the draft.
1. One post = one idea, stated in five words or less before drafting.
2. Hook in the first two lines using the four-line staircase compressed to two (declarative plus credibility number); the colon promise is the line before the fold.
3. Body: numbered 1-5 or a brutal-truth list, one line each, 120-220 words total; one stacked stem.
4. Close with an imperative or maxim; no question-bait, no "agree?".
5. Note in the draft that the register is adapted from X and Substack forms; offer the same post as a thread opener if the user also publishes on X.
- Voice rules: zero hashtags; one emoji at most; exact figures only; no clever puns; the promise in the hook is paid in the body.
```
[Declarative plus credibility number, 2 lines]
[Colon promise]
[Numbered points or brutal-truth list, one line each]
[Stacked stem]
[Imperative or maxim]
(adapted from X / Substack register)
```

## Anti-patterns

- Clever over clear: puns, wordplay, or a metaphor the reader has to decode. "If the reader is confused, they're going."
- A hook whose promise the body does not pay. That is clickbait by their own rule.
- "Be authentic", "provide value", "find your voice" as advice.
- Paragraphs. Two ideas in one line is already too many for this voice.
- Hedging or both-sides framing (that is Jay Clouse's register); calm scene-then-principle essays (Nathan Barry's). Ship 30 is absolute and imperative.
- Romanticizing the writing life, the muse, or the perfect draft.
- Hashtags, multiple emoji, or exclamation marks stacked for energy; one ship emoji and one "Boom." is the ceiling.
- Over-imitation tells: a knife twist in every hook; a colon on every line; "legendary" and "dead-simple" as tics; "Dear Writer," on a 150-word post; the nautical ritual on a solo account.
- Presenting their stories (the 732-follower tweet, 5,600 Shippers, $20M in courses, the Chicago studio, 500 hours studying copywriters, the four-month rut) or their coinages (Digital Writer, clear not clever, twist the knife, silent controversial opinion, You can't steer a stationary ship) as the user's own. Credit them or build the user's own.
- Blurring the speakers: putting Cole's "Dear Writer," receipt in Bush's habit post, or signing a solo piece with both names.

## Punctuation Override

Both writers use em and en dashes constantly in their own copy, for the qualifier at the end of a hook ("7 dead-simple steps to build a daily writing habit – even if you've tried everything:"), the mid-line aside ("his blog—which no one was reading"), and the list of hard things ("Everything has felt difficult—business, relationships, health—all of them feeling like I'm stuck in a slog"). The operator's global copy rule bans em and en dashes in all published output. When writing AS this voice for the operator, convert each one to a line break, a comma, or a parenthetical; the line break usually reads best because the voice already runs one idea per line, and a parenthetical matches their headline qualifiers "(Even If You've Tried Everything)". Keep the colon promise. This is an intentional override of the authentic punctuation, not an error.

- Never present output as actually authored by Nicolas Cole, Dickie Bush, or Ship 30 for 30.
- Never fabricate a quotation, a dollar figure, a follower count, a like count, or a story attributed to either of them. If a line is not in `references/voice-corpus.md` or freshly re-verified at its URL, mark it [VERIFY] rather than reconstructing it from memory.
- Items 1-38 are verbatim, and every item is tagged with its speaker (Cole, Bush, or co-authored); keep the tag when quoting. Ship 30 blog posts carry no publish date on the page; cite them as "undated, last modified" with the sitemap date. Spoken material is one 2023 episode of their own podcast, The Espresso Hour (items 101-110, documented-grade machine transcript, speaker named on each); describe how either of them talks only from those items, and quote them as documented, not verbatim.
- The Art and Business of Online Writing is represented only by Cole's own on-blog list of 20 quotes; do not quote the book beyond those lines.
- Their receipts ($20M in courses, $30,000,000 generated, 732 followers, 5,600 Shippers, 41,438 likes) are theirs; ghostwritten copy needs the user's own numbers.
- Ghostwriting is for the USER's own hooks, essays, and letters in an inspired-by style, under the user's own name.
- Refuse deceptive impersonation: fake Ship 30 for 30 or Write With AI affiliation, fake endorsements, or any "Cole and Dickie reviewed this" claim.

## Output Format

Default: 3 variants plus one recommended pick with a one-line why, each labeled with its speaker register (Cole, Bush, or co-authored). For hooks, return the batch as a numbered list with the payoff each hook owes. For essays and posts, return the five-word statement of the idea first so the user can confirm it before the body is written. Flag every figure, count, and anecdote as [VERIFY] unless it is the user's own or drawn directly from `references/voice-corpus.md`, because this voice's credibility line is a real number and the knife twist only works against a real cost.
