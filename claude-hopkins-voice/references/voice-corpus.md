# Claude Hopkins Voice Corpus

Research date: 2026-10-19

The excerpts below are short quotations from public posts, articles, and talks, included under fair use for style analysis and attribution. They are third-party content and NOT covered by this repository's MIT license.

**Provenance note.** All 33 items are verbatim from two public-domain books Hopkins wrote himself. Items 1-21 are from Scientific Advertising (1923), quoted from the full plain-text file of the archive.org item `scientific-advertising` (the Carl Galletti re-set "original text" edition, 21 chapters); 14 of the 21 chapters are represented. Items 22-33 are from My Life in Advertising (Harper & Brothers, 1927), quoted from the full OCR text of the first edition on archive.org (`mylifeinadvertis0000clau`) and cross-checked against a second independent scan of the same edition; the Preface and nine of nineteen chapters are represented. Line-break hyphens in the OCR were rejoined and nothing else was altered; every quoted sentence was checked against known OCR slips in those files and none were used. Hopkins died in 1932 and left no recording, so there is no spoken sample and no documented-grade section. Nothing here comes from a quote aggregator or a secondary summary. Because both books are in the public domain, these excerpts are verbatim rather than documented, unlike most classic-era voices in this repository.

**Punctuation note.** No em or en dashes appear in the sampled text. Item 18 preserves two spaced single hyphens as set in the archive.org edition ("And that's the way to answer them - not by arguments around a table. Go to the court of last resort - the buyers of your product."), where the 1923 printing likely used a dash. The operator's copy rule still bans em and en dashes in anything generated in this voice; see the Punctuation Override in SKILL.md.

## Verbatim excerpts

### Scientific Advertising (1923)

1. "The time has come when advertising has in some hands reached the status of a science. It is based on fixed principles and is reasonably exact. The causes and effects have been analyzed until they are well understood."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 1 "How Advertising Laws Are Established", archive.org `scientific-advertising`, 1923, https://archive.org/download/scientific-advertising/Scientific%20Advertising_djvu.txt, verbatim

2. "Therefore, this book deals, not with theories and opinions, but with well-proved principles and facts. It is written as a text book for students and a safe guide for advertisers. Every statement has been weighed."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 1, 1923, same URL, verbatim

3. "Advertising is salesmanship. Its principles are the principles of salesmanship. Successes and failures in both lines are due to like causes. Thus every advertising question should be answered by the salesman's standards."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 2 "Just Salesmanship", 1923, same URL, verbatim

4. "The only purpose of advertising is to make sales. It is profitable or unprofitable according to its actual sales. It is not for general effect. It is not to keep your name before the people."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 2, 1923, same URL, verbatim

5. "Ads are not written to entertain. When they do, those entertainment seekers are little likely to be the people whom you want. That is one of the greatest advertising faults. Ad-writers abandon their parts. They forget they are salesmen and try to be performers. Instead of sales, they seek applause."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 2, 1923, same URL, verbatim

6. "Don't think of people in the mass. That gives you a blurred view. Think of a typical individual, man or woman, who is likely to want what you sell. Don't try to be amusing. Money spending is a serious matter. Don't boast, for all people resent it."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 2, 1923, same URL, verbatim

7. "Remember the people you address are selfish, as we all are. They care nothing about your interest or your profit. They seek service for themselves. Ignoring this fact is a common mistake and a costly mistake in advertising."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 3 "Offer Service", 1923, same URL, verbatim

8. "People can be coaxed but not driven. Whatever they do they do to please themselves. Many fewer mistakes would be made in advertising if these facts were never forgotten."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 3, 1923, same URL, verbatim

9. "To reduce the cost of results even one per cent means much in some mail order advertising. So no guesswork is permitted. One must know what is best. Thus mail order advertising first established many of our basic laws."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 4 "Mail Order Advertising - What It Teaches", 1923, same URL, verbatim

10. "The purpose of a headline is to pick out people you can interest. You wish to talk to someone in a crowd. So the first thing you say is, "Hey there, Bill Jones" to get the right person's attention."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 5 "Headlines", 1923, same URL, verbatim

11. "It is commonly said that people do not read advertisements. That is silly, of course. We who spend millions in advertising and watch the returns marvel at the readers we get. Again and again we see 20 percent of all the readers of a newspaper cut out a certain coupon."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 5, 1923, same URL, verbatim

12. "Human nature is perpetual. In most respects it is the same today as in the time of Caesar. So the principles of psychology are fixed and enduring. You will never need to unlearn what you learn about them."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 6 "Psychology", 1923, same URL, verbatim

13. "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck. They leave no impression whatever. To say, "Best in the world," "Lowest prices in existence," etc. are at best simply claiming the expected."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 7 "Being Specific", 1923, same URL, verbatim

14. "Some advertisers, for the sake of brevity, present one claim at a time. Or they write a serial ad, continued in another issue. There is no greater folly. Those serials almost never connect. When you once get a person's attention, then is the time to accomplish all you can ever hope with him."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 8 "Tell Your Full Story", 1923, same URL, verbatim

15. "Changing people's habits is very expensive. A project which involves that must be seriously considered. To sell shaving soap to the peasants of Russia one would first need to change their beard-wearing habits. The cost would be excessive."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 10 "Things Too Costly", 1923, same URL, verbatim

16. "Advertising is much like war, minus the venom. Or much, if you prefer, like a game of chess. We are usually out to capture others' citadels or garner others' trade."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 12 "Strategy", 1923, same URL, verbatim

17. "The product itself should be its own best salesman. Not the product alone, but the product plus a mental impression, and atmosphere, which you place around it. That being so, samples are of prime importance. However expensive, they usually form the cheapest selling method."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 13 "Use Of Samples", 1923, same URL, verbatim

18. "Almost any questions can be answered, cheaply, quickly and finally, by a test campaign. And that's the way to answer them - not by arguments around a table. Go to the court of last resort - the buyers of your product."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 15 "Test Campaigns", 1923, same URL, verbatim (dashes as set in the source edition)

19. "To attack a rival is never good advertising. Don't point out others' faults. It is not permitted in the best mediums. It is never good policy. The selfish purpose is apparent. It looks unfair, not sporty."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 18 "Negative Advertising", 1923, same URL, verbatim

20. "We see countless ads running year after year which we know to be unprofitable. Men spending five dollars to do what one dollar might do. Men getting back 30 percent of their cost when they might get 150 percent. And the facts could be easily proved."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 21 "Good Business", 1923, same URL, verbatim

21. "Advertisers will multiply when they see that advertising can be safe and sure. Small expenditures made on a guess will grow to big ones on a certainty. Our line of business will be finer, cleaner, when the gamble is removed. And we shall be prouder of it when we are judged on merit."
    Scientific Advertising, Chapter 21 (closing paragraph of the book), 1923, same URL, verbatim

### My Life in Advertising (1927)

22. "This book is not written as a personal history, but as a business story. I have tried to avoid trivialities and to confine myself to matters of instructive interest. The chief object behind every episode is to offer helpful suggestions to those who will follow me. And to save them some of the midnight groping which I did."
    My Life in Advertising, Preface, Harper & Brothers, 1927, https://archive.org/download/mylifeinadvertis0000clau/mylifeinadvertis0000clau_djvu.txt, verbatim

23. "No; my only claim for credit is that I have probably worked twice as long as anybody else in this field. I have lived for many years in a vortex of advertising."
    My Life in Advertising, Preface, 1927, same URL, verbatim

24. "But the man who works twice as long as his fellows is bound to go twice as far, especially in advertising. One cannot get around that. There is some difference in brains, of course, but it is not so important as the difference in industry."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter One "Early Influences", 1927, same URL, verbatim

25. "I owe much to that condition. It took me among the common people, of whom God made so many. I came to know them, their wants and impulses, their struggles and economies, their simplicities. Those common people whom I know so well became my future customers. When I talk to them, in print or in person, they recognize me as one of their kind."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter One, 1927, same URL, verbatim

26. "A good article is its own best salesman. It is uphill work to sell goods, in print or in person, without samples. The hardest struggle of my life has been to educate advertisers to the use of samples."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter Two "Lessons in Advertising and Selling", 1927, same URL, verbatim

27. "Now college men come to us by the hundreds and say, "We have education, we have literary style." I say to them that both those things are handicaps. The great majority of men and women cannot appreciate literary style. If they do, they fear it."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter Two, 1927, same URL, verbatim

28. "All brewers at that time were crying "Pure." They put the word "Pure" in large letters. Then they took double pages to put it in larger letters. The claim made about as much impression on people as water makes on a duck."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter Seven "Medical Advertising" (Schlitz story), 1927, same URL, verbatim

29. "I came back to the office amazed. I said: "Why don't you tell people these things? Why do you merely try to cry louder than others that your beer is pure? Why don't you tell the reasons?""
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter Seven (Schlitz story), 1927, same URL, verbatim

30. "I told a story common to all good brewers, but a story which had never been told. I gave purity a meaning. Schlitz jumped from fifth place to neck-and-neck with first place in a very few months."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter Seven (Schlitz story), 1927, same URL, verbatim

31. "In a wide-reaching campaign we are too apt to regard people in the mass. We try to broadcast our seed in the hope that some part will take root. That is too wasteful to ever bring a profit. We must get down to individuals. We must treat people in advertising as we treat them in person. Center on their desires."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter Seven, 1927, same URL, verbatim

32. "Back of all of which lies the principle of personal salesmanship. All advertising should be based on that. Meeting a woman at her door is much like meeting her around her evening lamp. The same principles of salesmanship apply. And advertising is salesmanship-in-print."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter Twelve "Early History of Palmolive" (closing lines), 1927, same URL, verbatim

33. "I have always been an addict to work. I love work as other men love play. It is both my occupation and my recreation."
    My Life in Advertising, Chapter Nineteen "Some Things Personal", 1927, same URL, verbatim

## Derived stats (honest estimates from this corpus)

- Median sentence length: ~11 words; the characteristic unit is 4-9 words ("Advertising is salesmanship.", "Human nature is perpetual.", "Center on their desires.").
- One-line paragraphs: rare in the books; paragraphs are 3-6 short sentences stacked with almost no subordinate clauses.
- Hook-type frequency across the 33 items: flat law statement ~35%, parallel negation ("It is not for general effect. It is not to keep your name before the people.") ~20%, measured-result claim ~20%, homely simile ~15%, first-person case story (My Life in Advertising) ~10%.
- Connectives: "Thus", "So", "Therefore", "That being so" are nearly the only hinges; sentences starting with "And" or "But" appear once or twice per chapter.
- Numbers: cost-per-result figures in roughly a third of items (20 percent of readers, 30 percent vs 150 percent, five dollars vs one dollar, fifth place to neck-and-neck with first); percentages written as "per cent" or "percent" and small numbers as words.
- Imperatives: 1-3 per paragraph in the doctrinal chapters ("Don't think of people in the mass.", "Don't boast", "Go to the court of last resort").
- Similes: one per idea, always homely (water off a duck, used in both books; "Hey there, Bill Jones" in a crowd; broadcasting seed; war minus the venom; a game of chess).
- Emoji, hashtags, caps for emphasis, exclamation marks: zero across both books.
- Profanity: zero. Humor: zero by design ("Don't try to be amusing. Money spending is a serious matter.").
- Hedges: "usually", "in most respects", "for the most part" appear as calibration, never as retreat; claims are stated as proved.
- Favorite openers: a flat law ("Advertising is salesmanship."), a denial of a common belief ("It is commonly said that people do not read advertisements. That is silly, of course."), or in the memoir a first-person scene ("I came back to the office amazed.").
- Favorite closers: a one-line moral that restates the principle ("I gave purity a meaning.", "And advertising is salesmanship-in-print.", "And we shall be prouder of it when we are judged on merit.").
- Spoken vs written: no spoken record exists; every stat here describes the two books only. The memoir adds first person and reported dialogue while keeping the same cadence.
